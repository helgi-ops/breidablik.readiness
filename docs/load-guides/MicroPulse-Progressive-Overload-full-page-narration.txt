MicroPulse — Progressive Overload (whole page) — narration for Pictory
============================================================

Scene 1
Progressive Overload — the whole page, explained. It's the preparation-phase build plan: a safe, multi-week ramp toward match demand. Let's walk through it.

Scene 2
It's a safe, week-by-week build for every load KPI, from the squad's current baseline toward what a match actually demands. It plans weeks ahead — the macro planner, the preparation build, not a single day. And it's capped — two ceilings keep the build from running away, which we'll come to.

Scene 3
The verdict is one line: the squad can safely train about eight per cent harder week on week, with two players who have room to build faster and two who should hold steady. That's what progressive overload means — how much harder training can get each week without overloading.

Scene 4
The core is the ramp. Each load KPI builds week by week from now toward the match ceiling — here, total distance climbing from sixty-one per cent of a match now, up through the weeks to eighty-nine per cent by week five, approaching but never crossing the match line. Each cell is that week's per-player target, shown as a percentage of a real match, so the endpoint is match-ready, not arbitrary.

Scene 5
And it's not one uniform rate. Volume — distance and Player Load — ramps fastest, at eight per cent a week; you build the engine's size first. High-speed running ramps more cautiously at five per cent, and sprint slowest of all at four, with the tightest ceiling. That's deliberate: high-speed running is where hamstrings tear, on Malone 2017, so you build the top-end slowest.

Scene 6
Two ceilings keep the whole thing safe. The ACWR cap trims every week so the acute-to-chronic ratio stays at or below one-point-three — a week that would spike is capped back. And the match ceiling means no session exceeds match demand; once a KPI reaches match load, it holds there. So the build can't run away: it's always inside the safe range, and never past a game.

Scene 7
And it goes down to the individual, tuned to each player's current ACWR. Build faster — for the under-trained, ACWR below point-eight, ramp him up quicker. Progress — for those in range, follow the squad build. And hold — for anyone spiking above one-point-three, kept flat until he comes down, so his numbers stay level rather than climbing. The same squad ramp, individualized.

Scene 8
So, thirty seconds. First, the verdict — the safe weekly build rate, and who's flagged. Second, the ramp table — how each KPI climbs toward match. Third, the per-player column — build faster, progress, or hold. And remember the two principles: volume before speed, and capped at ACWR one-point-three and match demand.

Scene 9
The bottom line: Progressive Overload takes the squad from where it is now to ready for a match — a safe, injury-aware build, volume before speed, with two ceilings that stop it running away. It's the macro planner that gets a squad fit for the season.
