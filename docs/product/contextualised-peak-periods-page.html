Contextualised peak periods of play

Ju W, Doran D, Hawkins R, et al. Contextualised peak periods of play in English Premier League matches. Biol Sport. 2022;39(4):973–983. doi:10.5114/biolsport.2022.112083
A player's peak period is his most intense passage of a match (the hardest rolling 1, 3 or 5 minutes). Traditional GPS tells you how much he ran in it. Ju and colleagues synchronised tracking data with video across 50 EPL matches (583 player observations, 350 hours of coding) to answer what that running was made of tactically — and found peak periods are not mostly attacking sprints. They are dominated by Recovery Run and Covering out of possession (28–37% of high-intensity distance) and Support Play in possession (9–13%) — and the mix is position-specific.

The tactical taxonomy (Table 1)

In possession
Push up Pitch
Player moves up the pitch to play offside and/or squeeze to a higher line.
Break into Box
Player enters the opposition penalty box to receive the ball (typically from a cross — ball in front and wide).
Run in Behind / Penetrate
Player attacks space behind, overtakes and/or unbalances the opposition defence (typically ball is behind).
Over / Underlap
Player runs from behind to in front of the player on the ball or receiving the ball.
Run with Ball
Player moves with the ball — dribbling with small touches or running at speed with fewer touches.
Move to Receive / Exploit Space
Player moves to receive a pass or to create/exploit space (typically comes short or moves wide to receive).
Support Play
Player supports from behind/level, trying to engage in offensive/transition play (typically during fast transitions).
Out of possession
Interception
Player cuts out a pass.
Recovery Run
Player runs back towards their own goal to be goal-side when out of position.
Covering
Player moves to cover space or an opposition player while remaining goal-side.
Close Down / Press
Player runs directly towards the opposition player on/receiving the ball, or towards space or players not on the ball.
Unclassifiable
Other
All other high-intensity actions that could not be categorised by the above.

Why it matters for training

Because peak periods are position-specific and mostly defensive transition (Recovery Run, Covering) rather than attacking sprints, drills that only rehearse attacking runs under-prepare players for their real hardest minutes. A centre-back's peak is covering and recovering; a wide attacker's is moving to receive and running in behind. Ju et al. argue peak-period distance and action counts should shape position- and player-specific 11v11 drill design so the most demanding episodes of match-play are actually trained.

How MicroPulse uses this

This framework is the scientific basis for the Peak-period / Movement-signature read. MicroPulse can't reproduce Ju's hand-coded video taxonomy (it was coded frame-by-frame by analysts), so it approximates the same question — what is his peak intensity made of? — from data it already has: the Catapult IMA movement signature (forward / backward / lateral) plus the peak window's high-speed rate, and, where event data exists, the on-ball tactical labels in that same window (the physical×tactical Fusion read). The taxonomy above is labelled honestly as the inspiration, not a reproduction. High-speed running here is >19.8 km/h — the same threshold Ju used.
