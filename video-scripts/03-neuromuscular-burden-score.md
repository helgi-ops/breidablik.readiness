# Script 3 — Neuromuscular Burden Score

**Length:** ~2 min 30 sec voiceover (~400 words)
**Audience:** Coaches, S&C staff, sports medicine
**Tool:** Pictory (paste the voiceover blocks; use the visual cues as image / B-roll prompts)

---

## SCENE 1 — Hook (0:00–0:15)

**Visual:** Slow motion of a 100-metre sprinter exploding from blocks. Cut to the same athlete twelve hours later trying — and failing — to repeat the same sprint. Flashing text: "Same body. Different system."

**Narration:**
You can recover your aerobic system overnight. Your neuromuscular system needs longer. Much longer. Neuromuscular Burden Score tracks the load that doesn't recover by morning.

---

## SCENE 2 — Definition (0:15–1:00)

**Visual:** Animated diagram. The body is shown with two overlapping fatigue systems — aerobic (lungs, heart, blood) and neuromuscular (motor neurons, fast-twitch fibres, central drive). The neuromuscular system glows red after a sprint, then takes 48 hours to fade back.

**Narration:**
Neuromuscular Burden Score isolates the high-velocity, high-force actions that fatigue the *nervous system* — not just the muscle fibres themselves, but the brain's ability to recruit them.

It combines three things: maximum-effort sprints, peak accelerations, and peak decelerations. Anything that demands the highest neural drive — explosive, short-duration, all-out efforts.

The unit is composite — the score is normalised against the athlete's own personal peak history, so a value of one means "today was a typical day for this athlete," and three means "today was three times more neuromuscularly demanding than their normal."

---

## SCENE 3 — Why it matters (1:00–1:45)

**Visual:** Animated timeline. Day 1: high-intensity match. Day 2: athlete looks fine, jogs around. Aerobic recovery bar shows 90% recovered. Day 3: athlete tries to sprint — shows reduced peak velocity, longer ground contact time. A red label reads "neuromuscular not recovered."

**Narration:**
Here is the trap. After a hard match, an athlete's heart rate variability normalises in twenty-four to thirty-six hours. Their subjective wellness scores normalise. They feel ready. But their fast-twitch system — the part that fires the sprint, the cut, the jump — can take seventy-two hours to fully recover.

If you do another high-intensity session before that recovery completes, you don't just get suboptimal performance. You get an athlete sprinting on tired neural circuits. That's the moment hamstrings tear.

Neuromuscular Burden Score is the metric that prevents that compounding error.

## SCENE 4 — How MicroPulse uses it (1:45–2:10)

**Visual:** MicroPulse player card. A bar chart shows the last seven days of Neuromuscular Burden — Sunday match spikes the bar to 2.8. Monday and Tuesday remain elevated despite low training. Wednesday starts to drop.

**Narration:**
MicroPulse tracks Neuromuscular Burden across rolling seventy-two-hour windows. Even if today's training was light, the burden score keeps reading from the previous days. When it's still elevated, the coach sees a warning to scale back high-intensity drills — even if the player feels fine.

---

## SCENE 5 — Practical takeaway (2:10–2:30)

**Visual:** Coach planning the week, drag-and-drop high-intensity session. A warning pops up: "Striker A: neuromuscular burden still elevated from match. Move sprint work to Thursday."

**Narration:**
Neuromuscular Burden Score answers one question every elite coach asks: "Can this player handle high-speed work today?" Distance and heart rate can't answer that. The neuromuscular system can. Listen to it.
