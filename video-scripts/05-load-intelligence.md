## SCENE 1 — Hook (0:00–0:15)

**Narration:**
Most injuries don't happen during the sprint. They happen during the brake. Load Intelligence is the layer of MicroPulse that surfaces what most monitoring systems miss: the cost of stopping.

## SCENE 2 — Definition (0:15–0:45)

**Visual:** Four small panels appear on screen, each with an icon: a brake, a balance scale, an upward trending line, an equals-sign symbol.

**Narration:**
Load Intelligence is a set of four advanced metrics derived from Catapult GPS data. They're not raw counts. They're computed signals that tell you *why* an athlete's body is loaded the way it is. Decel Burden. Accel-to-Decel Ratio. High-Intensity Distance Trend. And Residual Decel.

Each one answers a different question. Together they tell you whether an athlete's load is the kind that builds fitness — or the kind that breaks tissue.

---

## SCENE 3 — Decel Burden (0:45–1:25)

**Visual:** Animation of an athlete sprinting forward, then slamming the brakes. Footage of the leg muscles in eccentric contraction during deceleration. A counter labelled "Decel Burden" rises sharply.

**Narration:**
Decel Burden tracks how many *high-intensity* deceleration events an athlete performed — specifically, decelerations beyond two metres per second squared.

Why it matters: deceleration is eccentric muscle action. The muscle is lengthening while contracting — the most damaging type of contraction for soft tissue. Critically — and contrary to a common assumption — the primary injury risk in decel is *not* the hamstring. McBurnie 2022 showed quadriceps activation reaches 161% of MVC during the mid-eccentric foot strike of a decel, while hamstring activation is *reduced* (−87% of peak quad activation). That quad-dominant + hamstring-deficient pattern, combined with sub-optimal trunk posture, increases anterior tibial translation — the mechanism behind ACL, quadriceps, and patellar tendon injury. Decel burden is more predictive of these knee-extensor and ACL injuries than total distance, sprint count, or even total deceleration count.

A high decel burden in a single session, or a sudden week-on-week spike, is the single clearest mechanical injury warning.

---

## SCENE 4 — Accel-to-Decel Ratio (1:25–1:55)

**Visual:** A see-saw graphic. Accelerations on the left, decelerations on the right. Balanced at 1.0 — a green tick. Then the right side gets heavier, ratio drops to 0.7 — a yellow warning.

**Narration:**
Accel-to-Decel Ratio compares how often the athlete accelerated versus decelerated. In healthy match play, it sits roughly at one — for every burst forward, there's a brake to follow.

When the ratio drops below 0.8, the athlete is decelerating more than they're accelerating — usually because they're being chased, scrambling defensively, or reacting to lost possession. Reactive decelerations are far less controlled, and far more injury-prone, than planned ones.

---

## SCENE 5 — HID% Trend (1:55–2:25)

**Visual:** A line chart over four weeks. The total distance line stays flat. But the "% at high intensity" line trends upward. A label appears: "Same volume, more intensity."

**Narration:**
High-Intensity Distance percentage — HID — is the share of total distance an athlete covered above their high-speed threshold, typically 19.8 kilometres per hour.

Tracked as a *trend* over four weeks, HID percentage tells you whether the athlete's training is drifting toward more explosive work even when total volume stays constant. A rising HID trend with stable total distance means the same hours of training are loading the body differently — and demanding more recovery.

---

## SCENE 6 — Residual Decel (2:25–2:55)

**Visual:** Equation on screen: *Residual Decel = Actual Decel − Predicted Decel (from Accel + Distance)*. A bar chart shows two players: one with low residual, one with high. The high-residual player's row flashes red with a small injury-warning icon.

**Narration:**
Residual Decel is the most subtle of the four — and possibly the most powerful. It's the gap between how much an athlete decelerated, and how much we'd *expect* them to decelerate based on how much they accelerated and how far they ran.

If a player's actual deceleration count is much higher than predicted, the difference is "residual" — extra eccentric work the model can't explain by normal running mechanics. In our data, residual decel has been the strongest single-session correlate of next-day soft-tissue complaints.
